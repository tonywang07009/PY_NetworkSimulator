# Evidence for tradeoffs

Use this when selecting a method depends on external evidence. Apply the relevant
parts before the tradeoff decision, or earlier when evidence changes the problem
definition. Reuse adequate evidence; do not impose an academic survey on a small
decision already answered by the project or platform.

Adapted from Peng Ming-hui's Graduate Student Survival Guide, chapter 12,
from the user-supplied PDF. This is a bounded methodological summary, not a claim
that every product decision needs an exhaustive literature review.

1. State the decision and the uncertainty that could change it. Derive initial
   search terms, then refine terms and synonyms from relevant sources.
2. Use surveys and representative sources to map competing approaches. Follow
   references backward and citations forward where available. Keep opposing
   results and record access limits; do not claim complete coverage.
3. Screen relevance and quality. For each useful method, identify assumptions,
   mechanism, application conditions, strengths, failure modes and limitations.
   Read beyond abstracts before attributing detailed claims.
4. Compare method and problem characteristics in one decision table: alternative,
   expected benefit, cost, risk, applicability, source locator and unknowns.
5. Explain which evidence applies to this project's constraints and what still
   needs a prototype, test or measurement. Agree the choice and acceptance;
   an appealing paper result does not prove local implementation performance.

Use primary technical sources, official documentation and standards as relevant.
Retain source title/URL or file, version/date, section/page and search terms in
the existing research or interview record. Separate author claims, inspected
evidence, inference and human decisions. If retrieval is unavailable, record the
gap and pause only the dependent decision. Do not invent citations or copy the
private PDF into the distributed kit.
